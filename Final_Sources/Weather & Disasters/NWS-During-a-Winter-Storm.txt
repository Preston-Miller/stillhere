DURING A WINTER STORM

Source: National Weather Service — What to Do If You're Caught in a Winter Storm
https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-during
Accessed: November 23, 2026

Use the section that fits your situation: inside, stranded in a vehicle, or
outside. Take care to avoid cold exposure, overexertion and carbon monoxide.

IF YOU ARE INSIDE

• Stay inside. If using a fireplace, wood stove, space heater or other heat
  source, take fire precautions and provide proper ventilation.
• When it is safe to go outside, check that a gas furnace vent is not blocked
  by snow. A furnace that vents through the roof may need to be turned off
  until snow has melted off the roof.

If your heat goes out:

• Close off unneeded rooms. Put towels or rags in gaps under doors and close
  blinds or curtains to help retain heat.
• Wear layers of loose-fitting, lightweight, warm clothing. Remove layers if
  you become too warm, to avoid sweating and becoming chilled afterward.
• Eat and drink. Food supplies energy for body heat; drink water or other
  non-caffeinated, non-alcoholic drinks to help prevent dehydration.

IF YOUR VEHICLE IS STUCK

• Stay in the vehicle. Wind-driven snow and cold can quickly disorient you if
  you leave it.
• For warmth, the NWS advises running the engine about 10 minutes each hour.
  While the engine runs, open a window a little for fresh air and clear snow
  from the exhaust pipe to reduce the risk of carbon monoxide poisoning.
• Make the vehicle visible to rescuers. Turn on the interior light at night
  while the engine is running, and tie a brightly colored cloth, preferably
  red, to the antenna or door. After the snow stops, raise the hood to signal
  that you need help.

IF YOU ARE OUTSIDE

• Find shelter. Try to stay dry and cover exposed skin.
• If no shelter is nearby, the NWS suggests building a lean-to, windbreak or
  snow cave to protect yourself from the wind. A fire can provide heat and
  attract attention; the NWS suggests placing rocks around it to absorb and
  reflect heat.
• Melt snow before drinking it. Eating unmelted snow lowers body temperature.
• Move your arms, legs, fingers and toes from time to time to help keep warm.
  Avoid overexertion, including heavy shoveling, pushing a car or walking in
  deep snow if you are not in good health. Heavy work in the cold can strain
  the heart, and sweating can lead to chilling and hypothermia.

IF YOU MUST DRIVE

• Slow down: roads that look wet may be slippery. Clear ice and snow from the
  vehicle before leaving.
• Tell someone where you are going and which route you plan to take. Bring a
  fully charged phone, car charger and emergency supplies.
• If you begin to skid, ease your foot off the accelerator and steer in the
  direction you want the front of the vehicle to go. With anti-lock brakes,
  apply steady pressure; do not pump them.
• If visibility becomes too poor to continue, pull over and stop until it
  improves. The NWS advises turning off the lights and setting the parking
  brake so another driver does not mistake your lights for a route to follow.
