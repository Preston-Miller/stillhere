# Prepare and decide when to leave

Status: initial draft · Checked November 16, 2026 · Household use

## Before an emergency

- Choose a meeting place near home and another outside the neighborhood. Record an out-of-area contact on paper.
- Plan two routes out and a destination that can accommodate everyone, including pets and people needing assistance.
- Assemble drinking water, ready-to-eat food, a manual opener, medicines, first-aid supplies, lighting, radio, batteries, chargers, sanitation supplies, copies of key records and cash.
- Start with at least one gallon of water per person per day for at least three days. Add capacity for longer disruptions, heat, health needs and animals.
- Keep essential items portable. Check supplies and contact details periodically.

## When conditions change

1. Check official alerts and identify the hazard before deciding whether to leave or shelter.
2. Follow evacuation orders. Leave before routes become blocked when possible.
3. Take medicines, essential equipment and the portable kit without delaying escape from immediate danger.
4. Tell your contact where you are going when safe to do so.

## Do not assume

Sheltering is not always safest; evacuation is not always safest. A wildfire evacuation and a fallout shelter instruction call for different actions.

## Supplies and remaining work

Keep paper maps and written contacts with the kit. This draft still needs household-specific transport, accessibility, pet and medical-equipment plans.

Sources: [FEMA emergency kit guidance](https://www.fema.gov/print/pdf/node/503732); [FEMA citizen preparedness guide](https://www.ready.gov/sites/default/files/2021-11/are-you-ready-guide.pdf). Checked 2026-11-16.

# Safe drinking water: treatment and storage

Version 0.3 · Checked November 16, 2026 · Detailed draft; see limits and detail ledger

## Quick decision

Use sealed drinking water first. Suspected fuel, chemical or radioactive contamination requires a different source: household boiling and disinfection cannot fix it. Follow local advisories. A clear appearance is not proof of safety.

## 1. Kill germs by boiling

Settle cloudy water and decant the clearer portion, or strain through a clean cloth or coffee filter. Bring to a rolling boil for one minute; above 6,500 feet, use three minutes. Cool and cover. Straining alone does not disinfect.

## 2. Bleach: use one complete method

Use the product's drinking-water instructions first. The following is the EPA 2017 method **only for plain, unscented bleach labeled 6% or 8.25% sodium hypochlorite**, suitable for disinfection. Avoid scented, color-safe or cleaner-added products. Use fresh bleach or bleach stored at room temperature for less than a year. Have a clean dropper and measured water container.

Settle/strain cloudy water first. Select one row and one concentration:

| Water volume | 6% bleach | 8.25% bleach |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 quart or 1 liter | 2 drops | 2 drops |
| 1 U.S. gallon | 8 drops | 6 drops |
| 2 U.S. gallons | 16 drops | 12 drops |
| 4 U.S. gallons | 1/3 teaspoon | 1/4 teaspoon |
| 8 U.S. gallons | 2/3 teaspoon | 1/2 teaspoon |

Double the dose if cloudy, colored or very cold. Stir; wait 30 minutes. If no slight chlorine smell remains, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes. Smell does not establish removal of chemicals.

Source: [EPA, September 2017, pp. 1–2](https://www.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2017-09/documents/emergency_disinfection_of_drinking_water_sept2017.pdf). Small-dose teaspoon conversions are not inferred. This table does not apply to different or unknown concentrations.

## 3. Other treatment limits

Follow tablet labels for water volume and full contact time. Iodine and ordinary chlorine are unreliable against Cryptosporidium; correctly used chlorine dioxide products can address it. Avoid iodine-treated water during pregnancy, with thyroid problems or iodine hypersensitivity; prolonged iodine use is unsuitable.

Ordinary portable filters may leave viruses behind. Match verified filter claims to the organisms of concern. Clear water and correct equipment operation matter for UV treatment.

Source for boiling, contamination exclusions and treatment limits: [CDC emergency water guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/), checked 2026-11-16. Its 6,500-foot boiling threshold is used here. CDC's broad 5–9% bleach table differs from EPA's concentration-specific table; do not combine their doses into a hybrid method.

## 4. Build a stored supply

Store at least one gallon per person per day for three days; aim for two weeks if feasible. Add water for heat, pregnancy, illness and animals. Four people need a baseline of 12 gallons for three days or 56 for fourteen days.

Use food-grade containers with tight lids; never reuse chemical containers.

### Sanitize an empty container — not a drinking-water dose

1. Wash with soap and rinse.
2. Mix **1 teaspoon plain unscented 5–9% bleach in 1 quart of water**.
3. Close and shake the container so solution reaches every interior surface.
4. Wait at least **30 seconds**, then empty it. Do not drink this solution.
5. Air-dry, fill with safe water and close tightly.

Label the date. Replace home-filled water every six months; follow dates on commercial bottles. Store at 50–70°F, out of sun and away from fuels or pesticides. Pour or use a clean scoop; keep hands out.

Source: [CDC emergency water storage](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/how-to-create-and-store-an-emergency-water-supply.html), June 27, 2025. Volume examples are arithmetic from its baseline.

## Boundaries and next details

This does not establish flooded-well safety, chemical removal, rainwater potability or suitability for infant feeding. Granular calcium hypochlorite and tincture-of-iodine recipes in the local EPA source remain explicitly pending. Filter pore-size selection, UV limits and original diagrams need dedicated device-specific cards.

# Emergency toilets, handwashing and surface cleaning

Version 0.3 · Checked November 16, 2026 · Temporary containment; local disposal rules required

## Quick decision

Stop flushing when sewer damage or failure is reported. Confirm utility instructions before flushing with carried water. Flooding and power loss can disable septic systems. A house's location on a hill does not prove flushing is safe.

## 1. Assemble a temporary toilet

Supplies: stable five-gallon pail or usable toilet bowl, two heavy-duty bags per lining, absorbent litter or sawdust, fitted seat/lid, spare bags, gloves and protected waste storage.

1. Locate away from food preparation; provide privacy and handwashing.
2. Line with **two heavy-duty bags**. Add litter or sawdust.
3. Keep covered between uses; supervise small children.
4. At least daily, close and remove waste bags to protected storage; replace liners. Change sooner if capacity or leakage requires it.
5. Keep waste secure from children, animals and food until approved disposal is available.

Do not assume bagged waste is accepted in household trash. King County requires an announcement authorizing that route; rules elsewhere differ. Its burial guidance is not permission to bury waste elsewhere. Do not put plastic bags into a sewer or septic tank.

Source: [King County emergency toilets](https://cd.kingcounty.gov/en/dept/dph/health-safety/safety-injury-prevention/emergency-preparedness/personal-preparedness/emergency-toilets). This version chooses containment, not latrine construction.

## 2. Set up handwashing

Keep safe running water, soap and clean drying material beside the toilet. A dispensing container can provide running water without a functioning household tap; collect runoff away from stored water and food.

Wet hands, apply soap and rub palms, backs, between fingers and beneath nails. Scrub for **at least 20 seconds**, rinse under clean running water and dry. Wash after toileting, diapering, handling waste or animals; before preparing/eating food; and before and after wound care or caring for someone with vomiting/diarrhea.

If soap and water are unavailable, use sanitizer containing **at least 60% alcohol**. This is a fallback, not a reason to omit handwashing facilities.

Source: [CDC handwashing](https://www.cdc.gov/clean-hands/about/index.html). The dispensing-container setup is an implementation suggestion, not a certified system.

## 3. Clean a compatible household surface

Remove visible dirt with detergent and water before disinfecting. Ventilate and use label-specified gloves/eye protection. Never mix bleach with other cleaners or disinfectants.

Follow the product label. If directions are absent, CDC's surface method uses regular unscented **5–9% sodium hypochlorite**:

| Batch | Bleach | Room-temperature water |
|---|---:|---:|
| Larger | 5 tablespoons | 1 U.S. gallon |
| Smaller | 4 teaspoons | 1 quart |

These are separately stated approximate recipes, not exact conversions of one another. Keep the surface **visibly wet for at least one minute** if no label contact time is provided. Make fresh dilution daily. Wash hands afterward.

This is **surface disinfectant**, not drinking water, hand sanitizer or waste treatment. Food-contact equipment may require another method and rinse step. Unknown-strength or splashless bleach is not a substitute.

Source: [CDC bleach cleaning](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/about/cleaning-and-disinfecting-with-bleach.html), April 24, 2024.

## Boundaries

Quicklime and burning toilet paper are excluded. Sewage cleanup, latrines, groundwater setbacks, diapers, menstrual hygiene and local waste disposal need separate procedures. Containment does not disinfect feces.

# Food safety: outages, cooking and floods

Version 0.3 · Checked November 16, 2026 · Selected detailed tables; not a complete preservation manual

## 1. Prepare and track the outage

Keep appliance and food thermometers, ready-to-eat food, manual opener, cooler and ice packs. Refrigerate at **40°F or below**; freeze at **0°F or below**. Record outage time; minimize door opening.

With doors shut, allow about **four hours for a refrigerator, 48 hours for a full freezer, 24 for a half-full freezer**. These estimates do not override measured warming. Transfer perishables to an iced cooler before temperature control is lost.

After four hours without power or another cold source, discard refrigerated perishables. Thawed frozen food may be refrozen or cooked if still containing ice crystals or at 40°F or below, subject to exceptions below.

Do not taste food to test safety. Smell and appearance cannot prove safety.

Source: [CDC emergency food safety](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/keep-food-safe-after-emergency.html).

## 2. Distinguish outage duration from time above temperature

A four-hour closed-refrigerator estimate differs from **known exposure at 40°F or above for more than two hours**. For the latter condition, FoodSafety.gov specifies:

| Selected refrigerated foods | Action |
|---|---|
| Meat, poultry, seafood, leftovers, soups, stews | Discard |
| Milk, yogurt, soft/shredded cheeses | Discard |
| Eggs and egg dishes | Discard |
| Cut produce, cooked rice/pasta/potatoes, pre-cut packaged greens | Discard |
| Hard cheese, butter/margarine | Keep |
| Uncut produce, bread without perishable filling | Keep |

“Keep” addresses temperature exposure, not flood or chemical contamination. Frozen ice cream and frozen yogurt must be discarded even if an ice-crystal rule seems applicable. Frozen vegetables have a separate six-hour threshold in the full chart. Selected rows do not cover every product.

Source: [FoodSafety.gov outage charts](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/food-safety-during-power-outage), reviewed August 8, 2024. Consult the full chart for unlisted foods.

## 3. Check cooking with a thermometer

Measure interior temperature; some foods need resting time afterward.

| Food | Minimum interior temperature | Additional condition |
|---|---:|---|
| Beef/lamb/veal/goat/bison steaks, roasts, chops | 145°F / 63°C | Rest 3 minutes |
| Pork steaks, roasts, chops | 145°F / 63°C | Rest 3 minutes |
| Ground beef/pork and similar ground red meat | 160°F / 71°C | Poultry rule differs |
| Poultry, including ground poultry and stuffing | 165°F / 74°C | All listed poultry parts |
| Leftovers and casseroles | 165°F / 74°C | Includes meatless casseroles |
| Egg dishes | 160°F / 71°C | Plain eggs: firm white and yolk |
| Fish | 145°F / 63°C | Full source adds seafood checks |

Cooking targets do not salvage food rejected because of unsafe storage or contamination.

Source: [FoodSafety.gov cooking temperatures](https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/safe-minimum-internal-temperatures), reviewed November 21, 2024. Ham and wild-game exceptions remain in the full chart.

## 4. Floodwater and preservation boundaries

Set aside flood-exposed food for disposal; intact-looking packaging may not be waterproof. Commercial-can salvage steps are pending. Home canning needs tested food-specific recipes, jar sizes, processing methods and altitude adjustments. Do not infer them from cooking temperatures or a preservation overview.

Remaining detail work: full flood salvage/disposal table, food-contact cleanup, full cold-storage chart, infant feeding, thawing, cooling cooked food and tested preservation procedures. See the detail ledger.

# Power loss and carbon monoxide

Status: initial draft · Checked November 16, 2026 · Household outages

## Immediate actions

Use flashlights. Check whether anyone depends on powered medical equipment or temperature-sensitive medicine; use their established backup plan early. Keep refrigerator doors closed and follow the food guide.

## Prevent carbon monoxide poisoning

- Run portable generators outside, at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, with exhaust directed away from buildings.
- Never operate one in a house, basement, garage or other enclosed space, even with doors open.
- Keep battery-backed carbon monoxide alarms operating.
- Do not use charcoal grills or camping stoves inside a home, tent or camper. Do not heat a home with a gas oven.

If a CO alarm sounds, or people develop headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness or confusion around fuel-burning equipment, move everyone to fresh air immediately and call emergency services. Do not re-enter until responders say it is safe.

## When to relocate

Move to a safe powered location when indoor temperature, medical-device failure or other conditions cannot be managed safely. Leave before batteries and transport options are exhausted.

## Remaining work

Electrical connections, transfer switches, battery sizing, fuel storage and medicine storage require separate equipment-specific guidance. This card does not authorize connecting a generator to household wiring.

Source: [CDC power-outage safety](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/response/what-to-do-protect-yourself-during-a-power-outage.html). Checked 2026-11-16.

# First aid: adult collapse and severe bleeding

Status: limited initial draft · Sources checked November 16, 2026 · Clinical review pending

## Start safely

Avoid traffic, electricity, fire and other hazards. Call 911 or the local emergency number for a life-threatening emergency. Put the phone on speaker and follow dispatcher instructions. Send another person for an AED and first-aid kit if available.

## Unresponsive adult who is not breathing normally

Gasping is not normal breathing. Start chest compressions in the center of the chest at 100–120 per minute, about 2 inches deep, avoiding depths greater than 2.4 inches. Let the chest rise fully between pushes. If untrained or unable to give breaths, provide continuous compressions. If trained and able, use 30 compressions followed by two breaths. Use an AED as soon as available and follow its prompts; keep everyone clear during analysis and shock delivery. Resume compressions when prompted.

Continue until professional help takes over, normal breathing returns, the scene becomes unsafe or you cannot physically continue. This section is for adults; child, infant, drowning and overdose procedures need separate cards.

Source: [AHA 2025 adult basic life support](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-basic-life-support).

## Adult CPR troubleshooting and AED handling

Use a firm flat surface where safe. Follow the AED's pad diagrams on bare skin. Wipe a wet chest dry; remove the person from standing water. Keep everyone clear for analysis and shock. Avoid placing a pad directly over an implanted device. Once prompted, resume CPR promptly; attaching an AED is not the end of care.

If trained breaths do not raise the chest, reposition the head and improve the seal before the second attempt. If neither breath works, resume compressions and look for a visible obstruction before attempting breaths again; never perform a blind finger sweep. If a second trained rescuer can help, switch compressors every one to two minutes with minimal pause.

Source: [Red Cross adult cardiac arrest](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/adult-cardiac-arrest). Full pad-placement illustrations and special scenarios remain pending.

## Life-threatening external bleeding

Continuous or spurting bleeding, a substantial blood pool, or shock signs require immediate help. Use gloves if available without delaying care. Find the wound and press firmly through gauze or clean material; do not repeatedly lift it to check. With an embedded object, leave it in place and press around it. Monitor breathing and responsiveness, and prevent chilling.

For life-threatening arm/leg bleeding, apply a commercial tourniquet following its instructions:

1. Position it **two to three inches above the wound toward the heart**, avoiding a joint or the wound itself.
2. Tighten and secure as the manufacturer directs. Pain is expected; the goal is stopped bleeding.
3. If fully tightened but bleeding continues, apply a second above the first, closer to the heart, following its instructions.
4. Keep it visible; note application time. **Do not loosen or remove it yourself.**

If bleeding stops with pressure, check circulation below the injury, secure the dressing, and recheck. If bleeding resumes under the bandage, remove the bandage but leave the wound-contact dressing and resume firm manual pressure. Detailed packing and special wound locations require a separate trained procedure.

Sources: [Red Cross severe bleeding](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/bleeding-life-threatening-external); [Red Cross tourniquets](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/articles/how-to-apply-a-tourniquet). The tourniquet instructions here apply to limbs, not neck or torso.

## Supplies and limits

Gloves, gauze, commercial tourniquet, blanket and AED access. This is not a complete first-aid manual. Choking, burns and fractures now have separate draft procedures in guide 09; their listed gaps still apply. Medical conditions, pediatric CPR and prolonged evacuation remain unfinished. No medicine doses or invasive procedures are provided here.

# Immediate actions for disasters

Status: initial action cards · Checked November 16, 2026 · Not complete hazard plans

## Earthquake

While indoors, drop to hands and knees, protect your head and neck, get beneath sturdy furniture if close by, and hold on. Stay away from windows. Do not run outside during shaking. Afterward, expect aftershocks and assess hazards before moving. Wheelchair users should lock wheels and protect head and neck.

Source: [CDC earthquake safety](https://www.cdc.gov/earthquakes/safety/stay-safe-during-an-earthquake.html). The local handout's “triangle of life” instruction was not carried into this draft.

## Flood

Move away from rising water toward safe higher ground. Do not walk or drive into floodwater; depth, current, road damage and electrical hazards may be hidden. Find another route rather than crossing a flooded road. Follow evacuation instructions.

Source: [National Weather Service flood safety](https://www.weather.gov/safety/flood-turn-around-dont-drown).

## Nuclear fallout

Get inside a substantial building promptly; choose a basement or central interior area away from exterior walls and roof. Stay sheltered for the first 24 hours unless fire, collapse or another immediate danger makes that location unsafe, or authorities direct otherwise. Listen for official instructions. Do not drive outside simply to reunite with someone who is already sheltered.

Sources: [EPA radiation preparedness](https://www.epa.gov/radtown/radiation-emergencies-and-preparedness); [CDC radiation questions](https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/faq/index.html).

## Remaining work

Dedicated wildfire, tornado and hurricane procedures; smoke protection; tsunami response; radiation decontamination; chemical incidents; damaged-building re-entry. The general preparedness guide cannot replace these hazard-specific decisions.

# Wilderness priorities

Status: conservative planning draft · November 16, 2026 · Technical procedures not yet verified

## When stranded

Pause and assess immediate hazards, injuries, weather, remaining daylight, location and equipment. Do not let panic turn a manageable problem into an exhausting search for a route you cannot identify.

- Address immediate life threats and protect against cold, heat, wind and wet conditions.
- Use carried shelter and insulation before attempting elaborate construction.
- Keep drinking water and food separate from untreated sources and waste.
- Preserve navigation information and make your position easier for rescuers to locate. Avoid unnecessary travel if it would increase exposure or make you harder to find.
- Use a written route and overdue-contact plan before future trips.

## Avoid improvising from incomplete references

Do not identify edible plants or medicinal herbs from this draft. It contains no identification images. Do not substitute the Army manual's old snakebite, burn or tourniquet advice for current first aid. Traps, weapons, river crossings and improvised climbing systems are outside this guide.

## Supplies and remaining work

Carry suitable clothing, shelter, lighting, map/navigation tools, water treatment, signaling equipment and first-aid supplies. Specific shelter designs, fire methods, rescue signals and climate procedures still need source verification and original diagrams.

Scope source: local Army-Manual.pdf, Chapters 1–3, 5–7 and 18–19. This is an original priorities outline, not a validated condensation of all 233 pages. The file is an undated abridged FM 21-76 reprint. A complete newer Army source should support the next revision.

# Choking, thermal burns and suspected fractures

Version 0.3 · Sources checked November 16, 2026 · Clinical review and original diagrams pending

Call 911 for life-threatening symptoms. Follow dispatcher instructions. These are distinct procedures; do not apply adult abdominal thrusts to infants or thermal-burn instructions to every chemical exposure.

## 1. Responsive adult or child choking

If coughing forcefully and able to speak/cry, encourage coughing and observe. Severe obstruction produces weak/no cough, inability to speak/cry, squeaking or silence, and possibly pale/blue skin. Call for emergency help.

1. Stand to the side and behind; kneel for a small child. Support the chest and lean the person forward.
2. Deliver five separate blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
3. If obstruction remains, stand behind them. Place the thumb side of a fist above the navel and below the breastbone; grasp with the other hand.
4. Give five separate inward/upward abdominal thrusts. Alternate sets until breathing/coughing returns or they become unresponsive.
5. Use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts during pregnancy or when you cannot encircle the abdomen. Exact chest-thrust placement needs a separate illustrated card.
6. If unresponsive, lower to a firm flat surface and start CPR with compressions. Before breaths, remove only a clearly visible object. Never blindly sweep the mouth.

Source: [American Red Cross adult/child choking](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/adult-child-choking). Self-rescue, wheelchair adaptations and post-event assessment need fuller treatment.

## 2. Infant choking: updated sequence and an unresolved visual detail

Infants are younger than approximately one year; this section excludes resuscitation at birth. For severe obstruction, use repeated sets of **five back blows and five chest thrusts, never abdominal thrusts**. The 2025 AHA public update specifies the **heel of one hand** for chest thrusts. The currently accessible Red Cross page still describes two fingers. We are not treating those as interchangeable.

The infant must be supported with head/neck protected and head below the chest when performing these maneuvers. Detailed hand placement and turning technique require a current illustrated instruction card before this subsection is complete. If the infant becomes unresponsive, begin infant CPR with compressions, following dispatcher instructions. Pediatric CPR is still a separate unfinished procedure.

Sources: [AHA 2025 pediatric BLS, foreign-body obstruction](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/pediatric-basic-life-support); [AHA 2025 update](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/updated-cpr-guidelines-tackle-choking-response-opioid-related-emergencies-and-a-revised-chain-of-survival); [Red Cross infant page showing the discrepancy](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/infant-choking).

## 3. Thermal burns and scalds

Stop exposure safely. Cool the affected area under cool running water for **20 minutes**, starting promptly; NHS guidance describes benefit when started within three hours. Remove nearby clothing/jewelry unless stuck. After cooling, lay plastic wrap over the burn if available; do not wind it tightly around a limb. Avoid adhesive dressings, creams, oils, butter and breaking blisters.

Source: [NHS burns and scalds](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/burns-and-scalds/), reviewed March 31, 2026. Twenty minutes is the selected thermal-cooling procedure, not a requirement to immerse the whole person.

Seek emergency treatment for large/deep burns, burns involving face/airway, hands, feet, joints, groin, circumferential burns, electrical/chemical injuries, or concerning illness in a very young, older or medically vulnerable person. A deep burn may have little pain; low pain is not reassuring. Watch breathing and responsiveness.

Use clean water. If unavailable, use a cool clean compress; do not apply ice. Keep the rest of the body warm, especially in children or with extensive burns. Do not scrub a severe burn. Electrical exposure requires power isolation before approach; small skin burns can hide internal injury. Chemical burns need substance-specific exposure control and a separate procedure.

Source: [American Red Cross burns](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/burns). Its thermal cooling range is 5–20 minutes; this guide explicitly chooses the NHS 20-minute method rather than presenting two competing instructions.

## 4. Suspected fractures and dislocations

Treat an uncertain bone/joint injury as a potential fracture. Movement or weight-bearing does not exclude a break. Let the person rest; do not straighten or test the limb.

Call emergency services for deformity, exposed bone/bleeding, coldness/numbness/tingling below injury, major trauma, head/neck/back/pelvic/upper-leg injury, shock, or unsafe transport. Compare the hand/foot beyond the injury for color, temperature and sensation.

For a closed injury, wrap a cold pack in a thin dry towel: apply up to **20 minutes**, stop earlier if painful, and wait **20 minutes** before repeating. Control bleeding from open injuries without attempting to push bone back into place. Monitor breathing and responsiveness; prevent chilling or overheating. Seek medical assessment even if 911 is not needed.

Source: [American Red Cross fractures](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/fractures). Splint construction, circulation checks after splinting, rib care and transport techniques remain pending; this is not a complete wilderness fracture protocol.

# Heat and cold illness: recognize, act and escalate

Version 0.3 · Checked November 16, 2026 · Clinical review pending; not a full environmental-medicine chapter

## Heat: distinguish exhaustion from a medical emergency

| Condition | Concerning observations | Immediate response |
|---|---|---|
| Possible heat stroke | Confusion, collapse or altered consciousness in heat; skin may be dry **or damp** | Call 911; move to a cooler place and start active cooling immediately |
| Heat exhaustion | Heavy sweating, clammy skin, weakness, headache, nausea, dizziness or cramps | Stop activity; cool surroundings, loosen clothing, apply cool wet cloths; sip water if fully alert and swallowing normally |

Do not give a suspected heat-stroke patient anything to drink. Do not wait for a thermometer reading before seeking help for confusion/collapse. For exhaustion, seek help immediately if vomiting, worsening or lasting more than **one hour**. Heat cramps require rest and water or a sports drink; get help if over one hour, on a low-sodium diet or with heart problems.

Source: [CDC heat-illness action sheet](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/media/pdfs/2026/09/Heat_Related_Illness-NEWLOGO.pdf). Detailed immersion technique, safeguarding the airway, fluid restrictions and pediatric variations remain to be authored.

## Hypothermia: cold can impair judgment

It can occur above **40°F** when wet from rain, sweat or immersion. Look for shivering, unusual tiredness, confusion, fumbling, slurred speech, memory problems or drowsiness. Infants may have cold red skin and very low energy. A temperature below **95°F** requires immediate medical attention; concerning symptoms also warrant action when measurement is unavailable.

Get medical help, shelter from cold, remove wet clothing and insulate with dry layers. Warm the chest, neck, head and groin first. Handle the person gently. Do not give alcohol; do not give drinks to an unconscious person. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, follow dispatcher-guided CPR rather than assuming death from the cold.

Source: [CDC hypothermia](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/index.html), August 25, 2026.

## Frostbite: protect numb tissue

Early redness/pain can progress to numbness, pale/gray-yellow skin or a waxy/firm feel. Seek medical care. Get out of cold, remove wet items and protect with dry coverings. Avoid unnecessary walking on frostbitten feet. Do not rub, massage, use snow, or apply a heater/fire directly: numb skin burns easily.

Source: [CDC frostbite](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/preventing-frostbite.html), August 25, 2026. Controlled rewarming, refreezing prevention, dressings and evacuation are still pending. This draft does not authorize a field thaw when refreezing is possible.

## Equipment and remaining work

Dry insulation, shelter, safe cooling water, towels, communication and a thermometer help, but do not delay urgent care while searching for equipment. Altitude illness, immersion rescue, trench foot, safe rehydration recipes and prolonged evacuation require separate detailed cards.

# Wounds, infection and tetanus assessment

Version 0.4 · Sources checked November 16, 2026 · Clinical review required

Scope: basic care of small external wounds after bleeding is controlled. Major bleeding uses guide 06. This is not surgical wound care, wound closure, deep foreign-body removal or antibiotic prescribing.

## Small-wound cleaning and monitoring

1. Wash hands and use disposable gloves if available. Avoid touching the wound with fingers.
2. Control bleeding with direct pressure before cleaning. Remove constricting jewelry/clothing if possible without worsening injury.
3. Check for contamination or embedded objects. Do not dig out embedded material yourself; arrange medical care.
4. Gently irrigate with saline or clean running/bottled water. Clean surrounding skin with soap and safe water.
5. Pat dry and cover with a clean dressing. Avoid contact with floodwater.
6. Recheck at least every 24 hours and sooner if symptoms worsen. Do not seal contaminated wounds, bites or punctures closed yourself.

Seek medical care promptly for animal bites, dirty punctures, embedded material, increasing redness/swelling/pain, drainage or fever. Confusion, breathing difficulty, severe discomfort, rapid heartbeat or clammy skin with suspected infection can indicate an emergency; call 911.

Source: [CDC Emergency Wound Care, PDF p. 1, October 2017](https://www.cdc.gov/disasters/hurricanes/pdf/woundcare.pdf). An offline copy is included in reference-sources. The 24-hour review interval does not mean waiting a day when symptoms change.

## Tetanus: details to bring to a clinician

Wound type and vaccine history determine whether vaccination or tetanus immune globulin is needed. Dirty/major wounds include punctures, bites, soil/feces contamination, crush injuries, burns and open fractures.

| Vaccination history | Clinician assessment trigger |
|---|---|
| Unknown, never vaccinated or incomplete series | All wounds |
| Complete series; clean minor wound | Last dose at least 10 years ago |
| Complete series; dirty/major wound | Last dose at least 5 years ago |
| Complete series and last dose under 5 years ago | Generally no additional dose for wound prophylaxis |

Immune globulin is a separate professional decision for certain dirty/major wounds, including incomplete history or severe immune compromise. Antibiotics do not replace tetanus prevention. Seek assessment rather than using this table to self-administer treatment.

Source: [CDC clinical tetanus wound guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/), September 8, 2026. This table is a referral aid, not a complete immunization protocol.

## Limits and missing detail

Dressing-change technique, irrigation volumes/pressure, eye injuries, bites/rabies, diabetic wounds, open fractures and delayed evacuation remain separate tasks. No antibiotic doses or improvised closure methods are supplied. A source's professional treatment recommendations are not automatically layperson procedures.

# Urgent medical conditions: recognition and first response

Version 0.4 · Sources checked November 16, 2026 · Clinical review required

Scope: recognize common time-sensitive emergencies and begin appropriate first response while arranging care. These sections do not establish a diagnosis. Use the person's prescribed emergency plan and the instructions for the exact medication/device. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, use the CPR procedure and dispatcher guidance.

## Possible stroke

Sudden balance loss, vision change, one-sided face/arm/leg weakness, abnormal speech, confusion or a severe unexplained headache can indicate stroke. Ask about balance/vision, ask for a smile, raised arms and a repeated sentence. **Call 911 immediately** for a concerning sign; do not wait for every sign to appear. Record symptom onset or the last time known normal. Symptoms that disappear still require urgent assessment. Use emergency transport rather than self-driving when available. This section supplies no aspirin instruction for suspected stroke.

Source: [CDC stroke signs](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/signs-symptoms/index.html), May 19, 2026. No fixed time window here should be used to decide it is too late to seek help.

## Possible heart attack

Chest pressure, squeezing or discomfort may persist or return. Pain can involve jaw, neck, back, arms or shoulders. Shortness of breath, cold sweat, faintness, unusual fatigue or nausea may occur, sometimes without prominent chest pain. Call 911 immediately; do not wait to be certain. Follow dispatcher instructions. If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, begin CPR and use an AED when available. An AED is not used merely because an alert person has chest pain.

Source: [CDC heart attack](https://www.cdc.gov/heart-disease/about/heart-attack.html), October 24, 2024. Medication choices and doses require a separate verified protocol; none are inferred here.

## Seizure

Clear hazards, ease a falling person down, cushion the head, loosen tight neckwear and time the event. Turn gently onto the side when feasible; stay until alert and explain what happened. Do not restrain, put objects/fingers in the mouth, or give food/drink before full alertness. Do not attempt mouth-to-mouth during active convulsions.

Call 911 for a seizure lasting **longer than five minutes**, repeated seizures, trouble breathing or waking afterward, injury, occurrence in water, first known seizure, pregnancy, or diabetes with unconsciousness. Follow an established rescue-medication plan if one exists and you are trained. After the seizure, abnormal breathing/unresponsiveness requires immediate emergency assessment and CPR as directed.

Source: [CDC seizure first aid](https://www.cdc.gov/epilepsy/first-aid-for-seizures/index.html), May 15, 2024. Five minutes is an escalation threshold, not a reason to delay for other red flags.

## Low blood glucose: awake and able to swallow

For glucose **below 70 mg/dL**, an adult following the usual 15–15 plan takes **15 grams of fast carbohydrate**, waits **15 minutes** and rechecks. Repeat if still low, following the individual's care plan. Examples include 4 fluid ounces of juice/regular soda or the labeled amount of glucose tablets/gel. Avoid relying on fatty foods such as chocolate for rapid treatment. After recovery, eat a balanced snack/meal with carbohydrate and protein.

Do not give anything by mouth if unconscious, seizing or unable to swallow safely. Call emergency services; prescribed glucagon is a separate rescue treatment. Young children may need less than 15 grams and require their own plan. Below 55 mg/dL or inability to self-treat is particularly concerning.

Source: [CDC low blood sugar treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/treatment/treatment-low-blood-sugar-hypoglycemia.html), May 15, 2024. This is not a guide for insulin adjustment, high glucose or ketoacidosis.

## Severe allergic reaction / anaphylaxis

Trouble breathing, tongue/lip/face swelling, shock or altered responsiveness after an allergen exposure requires emergency help. Hives may accompany the reaction. Call 911 and assist use of prescribed epinephrine promptly according to the exact product and training. Auto-injectors use the outer middle thigh, but hold times vary by product: do not universalize one device's seconds or dose. Note the time and monitor breathing/responsiveness.

If symptoms persist 5–10 minutes and EMS has not arrived, follow the emergency plan/dispatcher about a second dose using a new device. Improvement does not cancel the need for emergency evaluation. This section does not substitute antihistamines for epinephrine or authorize drawing up injection doses.

Source: [Red Cross anaphylaxis](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/allergic-reaction-anaphylaxis), checked November 16, 2026. Device-specific instructions still need attachment for any selected product.

## Suspected opioid overdose

An unresponsive person with slow/absent breathing, gurgling/snoring sounds or discolored lips may have an overdose. Give available naloxone promptly according to its label and call 911; have another person call immediately if present. Do not delay CPR for an unresponsive person not breathing normally while searching for medicine. Follow dispatcher guidance for breathing support/CPR.

A common naloxone response interval is **2–3 minutes** before another dose if normal breathing has not returned. Use the exact product's dose and a new single-use spray when indicated; do not extrapolate to other reversal drugs. If breathing normally but unresponsive, place on the side and monitor. Stay until help arrives: recurrence is possible even after an initial response.

Source: [CDC naloxone guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/overdose-prevention/reversing-overdose/about-Naloxone.html), May 2, 2024. A complete nasal-device administration card and rescue-breathing procedure remain pending.

# Diarrhea and oral rehydration

Version 0.4 · Sources checked November 16, 2026 · Clinical review required

Scope: fluid-loss first response, especially severe watery diarrhea. This does not diagnose cholera or specify pediatric fluid volumes. Infants, older people and medically vulnerable people need early individualized assessment.

## Start replacement fluids and arrange care

Severe watery diarrhea and vomiting can cause dangerous dehydration rapidly. Use a commercial oral rehydration salts (ORS) packet with the **exact safe-water volume on its label**; packets are not all formulated for the same volume. Do not mix a packet into a guessed cup or bottle volume. Begin ORS promptly and seek medical care for suspected cholera/severe illness, including while traveling to care if the person can drink safely. Some people need intravenous fluids.

Do not substitute sugary soda, juice or sports drinks for ORS in severe diarrheal illness. Continue infant feeding as advised; CDC specifies continuing breast milk or formula for babies with watery diarrhea. Do not dilute formula to improvise rehydration.

Source: [CDC cholera treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/cholera/treatment/), May 29, 2025. Cholera-specific treatment recommendations are not a diagnosis for every case of diarrhea.

## Emergency fallback when ORS packets are unavailable

WHO's emergency home mixture is **one liter of safe water + six level teaspoons of sugar + one-half level teaspoon of salt**. Measure the full water volume and use level, not heaped, spoons. This is a drinking mixture, not a surface-cleaning or water-disinfection recipe. Do not add bleach or medicines. Prefer packaged ORS when available because measuring errors matter. Do not force fluids into someone unable to swallow safely.

Get care rapidly for severe watery diarrhea, weakness or dehydration, especially in an infant, young child, older person or someone already ill. This recipe does not replace care or establish dosing for kidney/heart disease or fluid restrictions.

Source: [WHO cholera questions and answers](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/cholera-outbreaks), September 29, 2026. Prepared-solution storage limits, age-specific replacement volumes and vomiting/persistent-diarrhea pathways remain pending verified instructions. They are not inferred from this recipe.

## Supplies and scope limits

Store ORS packets, a marked measuring vessel, clean water and proper measuring spoons. Track intake, vomiting/diarrhea frequency and symptoms for handoff. Do not prescribe antibiotics or antidiarrheal medicine from this card. Bloody stool, persistent symptoms or inability to keep fluid down requires clinical assessment; a complete red-flag pathway remains an authoring task.

# Wildfire smoke, tornado shelter and winter stranding

Version 0.4 · Sources checked November 16, 2026 · U.S. public-safety reference; local instructions take priority

## Wildfire: evacuation and smoke are different decisions

Follow local evacuation instructions promptly; staying indoors for smoke is not a substitute for leaving a fire-threatened area. Check official alerts and air quality when available. Children, pregnant people and those with heart/lung disease or other medical vulnerability need particular protection.

For smoke when the building remains safe, reduce outdoor exposure and keep smoke out. A cleaner-air room can help, but it does not protect a building from fire. Avoid indoor pollution sources such as smoking, candles, frying/broiling or unnecessary vacuuming. Close windows while smoke is outside, without allowing dangerous overheating. Seek a safer cooled/cleaner location if indoor conditions cannot be managed.

Source: [CDC wildfire safety](https://www.cdc.gov/wildfires/safety/how-to-safely-stay-safe-during-a-wildfire.html), August 24, 2026. An N95/P100 is particle protection when properly fitted, not a guarantee of safe air or protection from all gases. Respirator selection and fit require separate detailed instructions.

## Set up a cleaner-air room

Choose a room large enough for the household, with few doors/windows. Keep them shut while outdoor smoke is high; run a correctly sized portable air cleaner without an ozone-producing setting. Use recirculation for compatible cooling equipment. A suitable central system may support a MERV-13 filter if the equipment can accommodate it; do not force an incompatible filter into a system.

Keep necessary medication, drinking water and activities in the room. If cleaner air and a safe temperature cannot both be maintained, relocate to an appropriate cleaner-air/cooling location when travel is safe. An air cleaner does not remove every harmful component of smoke and needs power and maintained filters.

Source: [EPA cleaner-air room guidance](https://www.epa.gov/emergencies-iaq/create-clean-room-protect-indoor-air-quality-during-wildfire). A downloaded 2026 EPA factsheet is also in reference-sources; its detailed filter sizing and maintenance material still needs a device-specific card.

## Tornado warning: get into the right structure

Move promptly to a basement, safe room or interior room away from windows. At school/work, use the designated shelter; avoid large open-span rooms such as gyms or cafeterias. A tent, shed or mobile home is not a safe tornado shelter. Plan access to a substantial building before severe weather arrives.

Vehicles are unsafe shelter. If a safe substantial building is reachable before the tornado arrives, use it. If trapped without access, local terrain and hazards matter: NWS describes getting low in the vehicle with head covered or seeking low ground away from it. Do not present a ditch as safe where flooding is possible. Do not shelter beneath an overpass.

Sources: [NWS tornado actions](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-during); [NWS severe-weather safety and overpass warning](https://www.weather.gov/lot/severeprepare). A vehicle contingency is a last resort, not a preferred route.

## Stranded vehicle in a winter storm

Stay with the vehicle unless immediate danger requires leaving. Walking into poor visibility can make rescue harder. Call for help and communicate location; make the vehicle visible. Conserve heat with dry layers and blankets.

If it is safe to run the engine, NWS advises about **10 minutes each hour**, with the exhaust pipe kept clear of snow and a window slightly open for ventilation. Recheck the exhaust before starting: snow can accumulate again. This does not make an enclosed or snow-buried vehicle safe to idle. Watch for carbon monoxide symptoms; use the CO procedure in guide 05.

Avoid overexertion pushing the car or shoveling; sweating and cold stress worsen exposure. Do not eat unmelted snow to hydrate; it cools the body. Shelter and heat-loss prevention come first.

Source: [NWS winter-storm actions](https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-during). Engine and vehicle-specific operating limits take priority; electric vehicles need a different energy plan.

## Gaps

Hurricane storm surge, tsunami response, wildfire escape when trapped, respirator fit, air-cleaner sizing, chain installation and electrical vehicle backup are not complete procedures here. These gaps belong in coverage routing, not invented answer steps.

# Radiation: shelter and remove external contamination

Version 0.4 · Sources checked November 16, 2026 · Public emergency guidance

Scope: fallout/external radioactive dust, not chemical-agent decontamination or treatment of internal radiation injury. Follow official emergency instructions and do not delay escape from fire or collapse.

## Initial shelter decision

Get inside a substantial building, preferably a basement or central interior room. Stay inside and listen for official instructions; the initial 24 hours are particularly important. If the building is threatened by fire, collapse or another immediate danger, move to safer shelter. Do not leave safe shelter merely to reunite with someone already sheltered elsewhere.

Source: [EPA radiation preparedness](https://www.epa.gov/radtown/radiation-emergencies-and-preparedness). Protective time varies with the incident and official advice; 24 hours is not an automatic all-clear.

## Remove external material

1. Carefully remove the outer clothing layer without shaking dust into the air.
2. Put it in a sealed bag/container away from people and pets.
3. If a shower is available, wash gently with soap and warm water; use shampoo or soap on hair. Do not scrub, scratch or scald skin. Keep cuts covered while washing.
4. If no shower, wash exposed skin at a sink; if no sink, use a damp cloth/wipe. Include hands and face, gently blow the nose and wipe eyelids/eyelashes/ears.
5. Bag used wipes/materials and keep them away from others.

CDC distinguishes water used for decontamination from drinking water: tap water may remain usable for washing even when bottled water is advised for drinking. Follow incident-specific instructions; do not generalize that exception to chemical contamination.

Source: [CDC self-decontamination](https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/prevention/self-decontaminate.html), April 15, 2024. This removes surface contamination; it does not reverse a radiation dose already received.

## Limits

Potassium iodide decisions, pets, wounds containing radioactive material, monitoring thresholds and contaminated-property disposal need separate verified guidance. Do not infer that a household filter or boiling can make radioactive drinking water safe.

# Detail preservation standard

Established November 16, 2026, after the first draft. This standard supersedes brevity as the primary authoring goal.

## Purpose

Produce an original, detailed reference with a quick-action layer. Remove repetition and unnecessary prose; do not remove information needed to choose, perform, check or stop a procedure. There is no target word count per topic.

## Two layers per topic

1. Quick actions: urgent decisions and immediate steps, with an explicit link to the detailed procedure.
2. Detailed reference: prerequisites, equipment, quantities, sequence, timing, checks, exceptions, failure handling and escalation. Keep crucial warnings beside the step they qualify.

A quick card is not evidence that the detailed topic is complete. The growing guide collection contains mixed draft/detail levels; no guide is assumed complete merely because a quick-action summary exists.

## Information that must be accounted for

For every applicable procedure, preserve or explicitly resolve:

- Intended situation, audience and exclusions: who it is for, when it applies and when another procedure is needed.
- Recognition and decision points: observable signs, thresholds and branches, including uncertain observations.
- Preparation: equipment, material specifications, protective equipment and conditions needed to begin.
- Exact quantities: concentrations, ratios, volumes, units, ranges and allowable substitutions. Do not round or convert without checking.
- Order and technique: numbered actions, positioning, handling, relevant anatomy and equipment settings.
- Time: duration, waiting/contact time, reassessment intervals and deadlines.
- Success checks: what should change, how to check, and what does not prove success.
- Failure response: what to do if the procedure fails or required equipment is unavailable.
- Exceptions: children, pregnancy, older adults, disability, medical conditions, altitude, weather and contamination type where relevant.
- Contraindications and hazards: what not to do, harmful combinations and conditions requiring a different approach.
- Escalation: when to stop, seek professional help, evacuate or follow local authorities.
- Aftercare: monitoring, storage, cleanup, disposal, documentation and follow-up.
- Visual information: table rows, diagram labels, sequences and identification features that text extraction misses.
- Traceability: source section/page, edition, verification date, conflicts and the reason for excluding or correcting material.

Do not manufacture a number or technique merely to fill a field. Mark it unresolved and research it.

## Source-to-guide accounting

Before declaring a topic complete, create a ledger row for each substantive source section, table and figure. Each row records:

`source | edition | page/section | detail | destination | disposition | verification | unresolved issue`

Allowed dispositions:

- Retained: rewritten accurately, including qualifications.
- Merged: duplicate information consolidated with a named destination.
- Corrected: replaced by stronger/current evidence; both versions and reason recorded.
- Excluded: out of scope, unsupported, unsafe or unusable; reason recorded.
- Pending: not yet checked, rewritten or visually inspected.

An inventory row for a whole PDF does not satisfy this ledger. Extracted text is an aid, not proof that tables and figures were captured. Keep original sources available internally; do not quietly discard them after rewriting.

## Completion checks

- Every in-scope source section has a disposition, not just a chapter summary.
- Numerical instructions agree across narrative, tables and quick cards.
- Each critical warning is attached to the relevant step.
- Applicable population and environmental exceptions are present.
- A reader can execute the intended task without guessing a missing quantity, sequence or success check.
- Unavailable evidence and conflicting instructions remain visible as gaps.
- Medical procedures receive qualified clinical review; that is distinct from editorial completeness.

New topics should use this standard before further compression. Existing quick cards remain useful navigation aids while the detailed references are built.


## RAG packaging requirement

The primary product is reference data for the offline model. Export intact semantic units with stable IDs, source metadata, eligibility, audience, review status and explicit scope. Carry crucial warnings and exceptions in every relevant retrieval unit. Retain a full parent for child search results; do not let fixed-size splitting detach table headers or prerequisites. Raw extractions belong in a separate review collection.
